      File 2 · An old, old festival

      From the library

      From Samhain to sweets

      Long before plastic skeletons and pumpkin-spice lattes, the Celts of Ireland, Britain and northern France celebrated Samhain. The festival, first held about 2,000 years ago on 1 November, marked the end of the harvest and the beginning of the Celtic new year. On the night before, people believed, the border between the living and the dead grew thin, and the ghosts of the dead returned to walk the earth. To keep these wandering spirits happy, families left food and wine outside their doors, and anyone who went out wore a mask so that the ghosts would take them for fellow spirits.

      In the 8th century, the Christian Church turned the festival into All Saints' Day, also called All Hallows. The evening before became All Hallows' Eve, a name that was eventually shortened to “Halloween”.

      In medieval Britain, the poor went from door to door on All Souls' Day, 2 November, asking for “soul cakes”. In return, they promised to pray for the family's dead relatives. This custom was known as souling. Young people, meanwhile, practised guising: they dressed up and sang, recited poems or told jokes in exchange for food, wine or money.

      The jack-o'-lantern has its own legend. A man called Stingy Jack was said to have tricked the Devil so many times that, when he died, he was forced to wander the earth with only a burning coal inside a carved turnip to light his way. In Ireland and Scotland people carved scary faces into turnips to keep him away. When Irish and Scottish immigrants arrived in 19th-century America, they discovered that pumpkins were bigger and far easier to carve.

      At first, American trick-or-treating was more about pranks and chaos than sweets. It only became the family-friendly tradition we know today in the 1950s. Now Americans spend up to six billion dollars on Halloween every year, which makes it the country's second biggest commercial holiday after Christmas.

          File 4 · Modals of deduction

          Detectives don't just guess. They deduce. Here's how to say how sure you are about the present and the past.

          15 min
          ≈ 100%must / must have + V3I'm sure it's true.
          “The thief must have known the house.”
          ≈ 50%might · may · could (+ have + V3)It's possible.
          “She might have seen something.”
          ≈ 0%can't · couldn't (+ have + V3)I'm sure it's impossible.
          “He can't have done it: he was outside.”
          Present vs past
          ModalPresentPast
          mustShe must be scared.She must have been scared.
          might / mayHe might know the code.He might have known the code.
          couldIt could be a trick.It could have been a trick.
          can'tThat can't be a real ghost.Rosie can't have taken it.
          -ingHe must be lying.He must have been lying.

          Пастка: протилежність must у дедукції — це can't, а не mustn't. Mustn't означає заборону. Також кажемо might not have (можливо, не), але can't have (точно не).
